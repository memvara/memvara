# Bitemporal memory, explained

Every fact Memvara stores carries two independent dates, not one: when it became true in
the world, and when Memvara found out about it. This page explains why one date isn't
enough, and walks through the case where the difference actually matters.

## Why not just one date?

Suppose you record a single "last updated" timestamp for a fact, the way most databases
do. That single date has to serve two purposes at once — recording when the fact changed
*in reality*, and recording when your system *learned about* the change — and most of the
time, those happen at the same moment, so a single date seems fine.

The trouble starts the moment they don't line up. If a customer tells you today that they
moved offices last month, the fact became true a month ago but you only learned it today.
Pick either date to store, and you lose the other one permanently:

- **Keep only "when it became true."** You can correctly say the customer's address
  changed a month ago. But you can't answer what your system would have told someone who
  asked yesterday — which is exactly the question an incident review needs answered.
- **Keep only "when you learned it."** You can say the record changed today. But you can't
  correctly absorb information about the past without either lying about when it took
  effect, or refusing to record when it actually happened.

Bitemporal memory isn't an enhancement on top of ordinary time-tracking. It's what
happens when you refuse to collapse two genuinely different facts into one date, because
doing so makes an honest late correction indistinguishable from a real, current change.

## The three ways to ask "what was true"

Because both dates are stored, Memvara can answer three genuinely different historical
questions, not just one:

```python
mem.get_all(valid_at=T)   # "What do we believe TODAY about how things stood at T?"
mem.get_all(known_at=T)   # "What did we believe AT TIME T, about how things are now?"
mem.get_all(as_of=T)      # Both at once — "What did we believe at T, about T?"
```

`as_of=T` is exact shorthand for asking both `valid_at` and `known_at` at the same value
of `T`, and it's the closest thing to "what did we think, back then."

## Walking through the case where they actually diverge

A customer moved to Berlin on 1 March, but didn't tell anyone until 22 March:

```python
mem.remember("user", "lives_in", "Rome",   valid_from=jan1,  recorded_at=jan1)
mem.remember("user", "lives_in", "Berlin", valid_from=mar1,  recorded_at=mar22)
```

Now ask about 15 March — a date after the move happened, but before it was reported.

```python
mem.get_all(valid_at=mid_march)   # -> ['Berlin']   correct, given everything we know now
mem.get_all(as_of=mid_march)      # -> []
```

`valid_at` says Berlin, correctly — with the benefit of what we know today, we understand
the move already happened by 15 March. `as_of`, on the other hand, rewinds the system's
*belief* back to 15 March, before the correction was ever entered — and the answer comes
back empty, because as of that literal moment, neither value was confidently held: Rome's
own record already carries the later correction's end date stamped on it, and Berlin's
claim doesn't exist there yet.

This isn't a bug — it's the honest answer to a real question: *what would this system
actually have told someone if they'd asked at that exact moment?* And the ten-day gap
between 1 March and 22 March is precisely the period during which anyone relying on the
system would have gotten a wrong or missing answer. Being able to see that gap, rather
than having it silently smoothed over, is the whole point of tracking both clocks.

## Getting a plain-English version of this

`ask()` composes all three readings — what's true now, what we believe was true then, and
what the system would actually have said then — into one narrated answer, and explicitly
calls out when the second and third disagree:

```python
mem.ask("where do they live?", at=mid_march).text
```

```
user lives_in: Berlin.
  On 2026-03-15 this store would have said Rome, and that is what anyone
  acting on it then acted on. The difference was recorded 2026-03-22,
  7 days after the instant you asked about.
```

Nothing here is generated by a language model — every date and sentence comes directly
from a stored column, which is what makes it impossible for this answer to invent a date
that isn't real.
